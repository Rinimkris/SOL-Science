About SOL and Project SunbYte

SunbYte Observatory Labs (SOL) is a workshop series run with the Space Universities Network and the UK Space Agency. It gets undergraduate students involved in solar physics and space engineering.

Project SunbYte is a student-led project at the University of Sheffield. It lifts a low-cost solar telescope on a high-altitude balloon, above most of the atmosphere.

What you will learn

  • How imagers, spectrometers and Fabry-Perot interferometers are used.
  • How to operate a spectrograph and why different light sources give different spectra.
  • The principles of solar observation with a ground-based telescope.
  • How Fabry-Perot interferometers measure solar features with sub-angstrom accuracy.

Three instruments

Imager
Takes a picture. Shows where things are.
Spectrometer
Splits light into wavelengths. Shows what the light is made of.
Fabry-Perot
Picks out a very narrow range of wavelengths. Shows fine detail.

Spectrometer: Integration Time

Integration time is how long the detector collects photons for each measurement.

Challenge: Find the integration time that gives a clear spectrum without clipping.

Key ideas

Short exposure: few photons, so the signal is noisy.

Long exposure: more photons, so the signal is smoother.

Clipping (saturation): if a pixel collects too many photons it hits its limit and the real peak height is lost.

Absorption lines are dips (the Sun). Emission lines are peaks (lamps).

Key ideas

Seeing: blurring of the image caused by moving air.

Extinction: light lost as it passes through the atmosphere.

Light pollution: artificial light that brightens the sky and hides faint objects.

Moonlight: a bright moon also brightens the sky and reduces contrast.

Why a balloon? Project SunbYte lifts its telescope above most of the atmosphere, so there is far less extinction and distortion. Try the balloon altitude in Part B.

Fabry-Perot Interferometer

What is it?

Two parallel, partially reflecting plates, separated by a very small distance.

Look at the zig-zag in the diagram. The light reflects back and forth between the plates, and a little escapes each time. All the escaping beams interfere. This gives very narrow transmission peaks.

Why is this useful? In solar observations we need sub-angstrom spectral resolution (1 angstrom = 0.1 nm) to examine very narrow absorption or emission features.

The equation

A bright peak happens when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths.

2 n d cos(θ) = m λ

n = refractive index between the plates

d = distance between the plates

θ = angle of the light inside the cavity

m = interference order (a whole number)

λ = wavelength of the light

Change the wavelength it selects

So the Fabry-Perot can select a very narrow range of wavelengths. How do we change which wavelength it selects? Use the sliders and watch the peaks move.

The rings you would see

Bright: waves add up (constructive interference). Dark: waves cancel (destructive interference).

Reflectivity: higher reflectivity means more bounces, so the peaks and rings get sharper.
